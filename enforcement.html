Two legal layers — plus a cryptographic third, where the product is a signing record

No single instrument can stop a sale — a private company’s owners can always vote to change its governing documents. So the Standard’s own enforcement is two legal layers, each thin and swappable: a charter-level bar and a per-signee remedy. Where the certified product is itself a signing record (e.g. WorkSigned), a third, cryptographic layer — the record’s durability — applies, and it is the real long-term answer that a capture cannot touch.

The cryptographic third (where the product is a signing record, e.g. WorkSigned)

Applies where the certified product is a signing record: the record is a hash chain signed against a public chain — and, in the institution-hosted model, against your own key. A change in ownership, a sale, or even the issuer’s failure does not invalidate, re-price, or re-purpose what has already been signed. This is mathematical, not legal — and it is the layer a capture cannot touch.

Layer 1 — The bar (charter + stockholders’ agreement)

The company’s charter and stockholders’ agreement require a supermajority and a right of first refusal for any sale of control, the core IP, or the brand/seal to a private-equity, venture, or for-profit acquirer — and any material weakening of the Equitable Terms. A capture is therefore priced and public, not possible to do quietly.

Layer 2 — The remedy (per-signee right)

Each signee holds a non-discretionary right in the service agreement: a material adverse change to their Equitable Terms (a capture, an IP/brand sale, or a weakening of data ownership, auditability, or the no-extraction rule) triggers termination, data return, escrow release — and their records remain valid. It is a breach remedy that deters, not a bar that blocks — the bar is layer 1.

The Equitable Principles (the constant)

These are what a company commits to. They are the product — stable and public — independent of any enforcement mechanism.

Data stays yours

Your data and your records are owned by you (or your institution). Never sold, never mined, never used to train models. The company may process them for the service, not own them.

No extraction

The value you create is not siphoned into the issuer’s or a third party’s profit. No data selling, no brand-licensing for third-party profit, no mining.

No capture

No change of control, sale of the core IP, or sale of the brand/seal to a private-equity, venture, or for-profit acquirer. Permitted acquirers only: a mission-aligned for-profit research institution that adopts the principles in writing, a founder- and staff-directed buyout, or dissolution into a non-profit / research-purpose trust.

Checkable, not a label

The deployed code is auditable by your IT security team; the company’s own commitments are checkable, not just claimed. Verifiability is the whole point.

The bar — what the charter actually says

The honest version of “it can’t be sold to PE”: a private company’s owners can, with enough votes, amend its charter. What the charter does is make capture expensive, public, and contractually accountable — and make the record’s validity independent of it.

Supermajority + ROFR on the big decisions

The charter requires a supermajority vote (and a right of first refusal) for: (i) a change of control / merger to a Prohibited Acquirer, (ii) a sale of substantially all assets, (iii) a sale or an exclusive, for-profit license of the core IP, and (iv) a sale or an exclusive, for-profit license of the brand/seal. Non-exclusive, open-source, and community-benefit licenses are permitted — the software is meant to be shared.

Can only be strengthened, never weakened

The Equitable Terms may only be strengthened, never weakened, during the term; a material adverse change to them (weakening data ownership, enabling sale/mining of the signee’s data or records, reducing auditability) requires the same supermajority. This is the legal form of the “only ever gets stronger” seal — not the over-claim that “any attempt is void.” A court reforms an overbroad restriction; a narrow, defined one is what holds.

The core IP and the brand/seal are defined in the charter. The “exclusive + for-profit” test is objective (read the license terms and the licensee’s status) — not a motive question — so it can’t be dodged by a for-profit taking an exclusive license to itself.

The remedy — what a signee can actually do

A sale to PE and a change to a data or auditability policy are the same kind of decision — both change something that affects the customer without their consent. The signee’s right covers both, and it is a remedy, not a bar: it makes a capture or an adverse change costly (a wall of breach claims, priced into any deal), not impossible.

Non-discretionary

The right triggers on an objective event — a Prohibited-Acquirer sale, or a material adverse Equitable-Terms change. It is not a case-by-case “consent” gate, so a single institution cannot hold a sale or a policy change for ransom.

A bargained remedy, not a gift

It is a termination + data-return + escrow-release right, tied to the service relationship, that lapses when the signee’s service ends. That keeps it a service-contract right (not a security) and a bargained remedy (not a gratuitous veto a court would reform out).

The records survive it (where the product is a signing record)

Where the service produces a signing record (e.g. WorkSigned), a signee who terminates still has records that verify independently of the company’s future ownership or survival. The remedy protects the relationship; the cryptography protects the record.

If the deal goes wrong, you keep the software

Four objective events — and only those. If any of them happens, your license becomes perpetual, irrevocable, and free (at the last agreed price, or $0), and you can keep running the software. No renegotiation, no consent, no case-by-case call.

  • Pricing rises. The price is fixed — e.g. a set annual fee, pegged to inflation only, locked in at the first agreement. It can’t be raised past that.
  • The data policy changes. A weakening of the data rules — your ownership, the right to sell or mine your data, or auditability.
  • Ownership changes. A sale or change of control to a private equity firm, a venture capital fund, or any acquirer that would control the board.
  • The company ceases. WorkSigned stops operating, for any reason.

Two things follow from that. First, every institution still using the software keeps using it for free — so a buyer can’t count on the customer base, which is the thing that makes the company valuable. Second, because the signing is a network, not one company, the other trusted authorities (e.g. approved institutions) carry on signing and verifying — the trust doesn’t depend on any single company staying alive. And on cessation, the software open-sources under the MIT license — so it can never be taken off the market, and anyone can run it, audit it, or keep it going.

Data processing & continuity

Data processing

Research content stays on the institution’s servers. It is not used to train models. The LLM backends (your hosted LLMs, or WorkSigned’s private LLMs, or your own endpoint) are the only AI subprocessors that touch AI-assisted work. Breach notification, return, and deletion are in the service agreement’s protected terms.

Continuity

The service commitment runs for the life of the company, so it does not lapse on a renewal. If the company ceases, the continuity plan applies: the signed record (where the product is one) and its verification material are preserved so the record remains checkable, and the client’s data is returned in a defined format with a deletion attestation.

The principles, durable by design

Adopt the Equitable Principles. The bar and the remedy make capture costly and accountable; where the product is a signing record (e.g. WorkSigned), the record’s validity is cryptographic and outlives any change of ownership. We don’t claim a sale is impossible — we make it expensive, public, and contractually answerable.
