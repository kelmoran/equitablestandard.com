A standard for a different kind of company

The Equitable Standard

Much of today's tech is built on extraction — your data, attention, and effort used to generate value that flows away from you. The Equitable Standard defines a different kind of corporation: your data is never sold, and a charter-level bar on sale or change of control to a private equity firm, a venture capital fund, or any acquirer that would control the board — sealed by a supermajority and a per-signee remedy, so a capture is priced and public, not possible to do quietly. The rules can only be strengthened, never weakened.

What the Equitable Standard is

A company that keeps its promises. Three short sections: what it promises, how that promise is enforced, and how the gaps are closed.

What is the promise

  • Your data stays yours. We don't sell, mine, or use your data or your work to train anything. It's only ever used to give you the service you asked for.
  • We can't be bought up. The company can't be sold to a private equity firm, a venture capital fund, or a buyer who would take control of the board. Whoever runs it has to follow the same rules.
  • We can't change the rules to weaken you. The data, privacy, and audit rules can only get stronger, never weaker. Making them weaker is treated the same as selling the company to a buyer — it needs the same supermajority vote.
  • You can check us. Your IT security team can audit the deployed code, and our finances and cap table are public. Trust comes from checking, not from us saying so.
  • If we ever break this: if we were sold to one of those buyers, or if we tried to weaken your data or auditability terms, you could end your account and get your data back. And what you've signed still holds — its validity doesn't depend on who we are.

What are the enforcement mechanisms

  • The charter. The company's own governing document requires a supermajority (not a normal majority) for a sale or change of control, a sale of the core IP, or a sale of the brand — and gives users and the public a right of first refusal, so they can always buy it before anyone else.
  • Your right to exit. If any of the protected events happens, the service agreement with every user is breached. Each user can then cancel, get their data back, and keep their signed work valid. We don't claim a sale is impossible — we make it costly and public, because a buyer who would face claims from every user at once is a buyer who won't show up.
  • The signed record. (For signing products like WorkSigned.) Every record is a chain of hashes signed against a public chain — or, in the institution-hosted model, against your own key. A sale or even the company going out of business cannot change, reprice, or reuse anything already signed. That part is math, not law.

How are loopholes sealed

  • "Just change the charter" — sealed by the supermajority. The founders can't amend these provisions alone. A normal majority can't touch them; only the stated supermajority can, and a change that weakens the terms needs the same vote as a sale.
  • "Sell the assets instead of the company" — sealed by the asset and IP rules. Selling substantially all of the company's assets, or the core IP or the brand/seal to a for-profit buyer, is covered by the same supermajority + right of first refusal as a change of control. You can't dodge the rule by selling the pieces.
  • "Quietly change our policy" — sealed by treating it as a protected event. A change to how we use, sell, or make less auditable your data is a protected event, not a routine policy update. It needs the supermajority and it triggers your exit right.
  • "Buy us after a quiet change" — sealed by the exit right. Even if a weakening slipped through, every user can leave and take their data with them. A buyer can't count on the user base — the thing that makes the company valuable — sticking around.
  • "Let the contract lapse at renewal" — sealed by continuity. The data and auditability commitments run for the life of the company; they don't reset every year. And the signed record's validity is independent of the company's survival, so it can't be quietly let go.

Certification

The Equitable Standard isn't exclusive to any one company. Any corporation can commit to it and be certified.

Who can apply

Any incorporated entity — startups, existing corporations, and cooperatives. The only requirement is a genuine commitment to the standard's principles: no data sharing, no sale to a private equity firm, a venture capital fund, or any acquirer that would control the board, locked core rules, transparency, and independence.

What it means

Certified companies receive a publicly verifiable seal and are listed in a public registry. The seal signals to users, investors, and partners that value flows to creators, not extractors. Compliance is reviewed periodically, and the core terms can only be strengthened, never weakened — a material adverse change requires the stated supermajority and triggers the per-signee remedy, so the commitment cannot be quietly walked back.

How to apply

Three steps: submit your basic information and a statement of commitment, undergo a compliance review by the Equitable Standard board, and if approved, receive the seal and a registry listing. The review takes about 30 days, and applying is free.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Equitable Standard?

A set of governance rules that define what an equitable corporation is. Any company can apply to meet the standard and earn certification. The rules cover no data sharing, no sale to a private equity firm, a venture capital fund, or any acquirer that would control the board, locked core rules, transparency, and independence.

Can the company be sold to an investor or acquirer?

Not quietly — the charter requires a supermajority and a right of first refusal for any sale or change of control to a private equity firm, a venture capital fund, or any acquirer that would control the board, so a capture is priced and public, not possible to do quietly.

Why not just a social-purpose corporation or a non-profit?

SPCs are generic and vague — the label tells users nothing concrete. Non-profits cap the founder's earning potential and can't compete the way a for-profit can. The Equitable Standard is different: it locks in no data sharing, requires a supermajority and right of first refusal for any sale to a private equity firm, a venture capital fund, or any acquirer that would control the board, and makes the commitments verifiable — a legal commitment with real teeth, not a marketing label, where the terms can only be strengthened.

Should we make our whole company transparent? No trade secrets?

No. The Standard doesn't require you to abandon business secrets — you need them to be profitable. The transparency it demands is about governance, finances, and data practices, not your product or strategy. Protect your IP; be open about how you run the company.

What backs the commitments — is it enforceable?

Everything is enforced by the same two layers: the charter-level bar (supermajority + ROFR on a capture, an IP/brand sale, or any material weakening of the terms), and the per-signee remedy that a capture or an adverse change breaches. So no future board, owner, or SLA can quietly weaken the structural commitments — no sale to PE/VC, the data rules, the locked provisions. One path to enforcement, and it only ever gets stronger. (Where the product is a signing record, e.g. WorkSigned, a third, cryptographic layer applies.)

How much does certification cost?

Applying is free. Certification is 1% of distributable profits annually, capped (and adjusted for inflation), with no setup or hidden fees. You only pay when profitable — bad years can't cripple the business. The core terms can only be strengthened, never weakened: a material adverse change requires the stated supermajority and triggers the per-signee remedy, so the commitment cannot be quietly walked back.

Is there anything wrong with private equity or venture capital?

Nothing is inherently wrong — they're valid, time-tested instruments. But under the Standard, they cannot buy the company or gain control. The charter requires a supermajority and a right of first refusal for any sale to a private equity firm, a venture capital fund, or any acquirer that would control the board, so founders and users don't have to give up the company, its data, or its vision to raise capital.
